This one assumes you already know how salary sacrifice works — if not, [Cut Your Income Tax with Pension Salary Sacrifice](/resources/salary-sacrifice-pension-tax-guide) covers the mechanics first. This is about the decisions on top of that: *when* to increase what you're sacrificing, and a rule that lets some people pay in far more in a single year than the standard annual allowance suggests.

As always: general information, not personal advice. Pension rules have real teeth if you get them wrong — a proper conversation with a financial adviser is worth it once amounts get large.

## When to increase your salary sacrifice percentage

The easiest moment to increase a sacrifice is exactly when you'd otherwise never notice the money: a payrise or bonus. Sacrificing some or all of an increase before it ever hits your take-home pay means you never adjust your spending to it in the first place — no lifestyle inflation to fight against later, and the tax and National Insurance saving happens automatically.

This is especially valuable if a payrise or bonus pushes your income into the £100,000–£125,140 band, where the tapering Personal Allowance creates an effective marginal rate around 60%. Sacrificing enough to pull your taxable income back under £100,000 restores some or all of that lost allowance — see the salary sacrifice guide linked above for the exact mechanics, but the short version is: this is one of the highest-value adjustments available if your income sits in that range.

Bonuses specifically are worth a second look, because many employers allow sacrificing all or part of an annual bonus directly into your pension before it's paid — same Income Tax and National Insurance savings apply, and it's easy to overlook because a bonus already feels like "extra" money that doesn't get the same scrutiny as regular salary.

## The carry-forward rule most people don't know about

The Annual Allowance — the most you can pay into pensions each year with tax relief — is £60,000 for 2026/27 (or 100% of your earnings if lower). What's less well known: if you were a member of a registered pension scheme in each of the previous three tax years and didn't use your full allowance in some of them, you can carry that unused amount forward and add it to this year's allowance.

Practically, that means someone who's been under-contributing for a few years — perhaps because they were younger, earned less, or simply hadn't got round to increasing contributions — could potentially pay in considerably more than £60,000 in a single year without triggering an annual allowance tax charge, provided they have enough UK earnings that year to support the contribution (carry-forward raises your allowance, but a personal contribution still can't exceed 100% of your relevant UK earnings in the year you actually pay it in).

This tends to matter most around a specific event: a large bonus, an inheritance, or the proceeds from selling a property or a business. Rather than that money sitting in a GIA and getting taxed on its growth, carry-forward can let you shelter a much larger slice of it inside a pension in one go than the standard allowance would otherwise allow.

Two things to check before relying on this:

- **You need to have been a pension scheme member in the years you're carrying forward from** — not necessarily contributing, but a member (active, deferred, or pension credit member all count).
- **You can't use carry-forward if you've already triggered the Money Purchase Annual Allowance (MPAA)** — this happens once you've started flexibly drawing income from a defined contribution pension, and it caps further contributions at a much lower figure regardless of unused allowance from previous years.

## Other pension checks worth making

A few lower-effort optimisations that are easy to forget about entirely:

- **Old workplace pensions from previous employers.** These are often still sitting in a default fund chosen by the scheme provider, sometimes with higher charges than you'd accept if you were choosing today. Worth checking the fund and fee — consolidating into a SIPP via one of the platforms in [The Top 5 UK Investment Platforms, Compared](/resources/best-uk-investment-platforms-compared) can mean more control and lower ongoing charges, though it's worth checking first whether the old scheme has anything valuable attached (a guaranteed annuity rate, for instance) that would be lost by transferring.
- **Whether your employer passes back their own National Insurance saving.** Your employer also saves National Insurance on whatever you sacrifice — some pass some or all of that saving back into your pension as an extra employer contribution, but it's rarely automatic. A five-minute question to HR that can meaningfully add up over decades.
