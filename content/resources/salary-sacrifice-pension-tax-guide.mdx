If your employer offers salary sacrifice for pension contributions and you're not using it, you're very likely leaving money on the table — not through some clever loophole, but through simple mechanics baked into how UK tax and National Insurance work.

This one's worth understanding properly rather than just ticking the box on an HR form, because how much it saves you depends heavily on where your income sits — and for some earners, it's worth dramatically more than others.

As always: this explains how the rules work in general terms, not what you personally should do. Your payslip, employer scheme rules, and broader financial picture all matter, and for anything beyond the basics it's worth a conversation with a financial adviser or accountant.

## What salary sacrifice actually is

Normally, when you pay into a workplace pension, the money comes out of your salary *after* it's already been paid to you (or via "net pay" arrangements where it's deducted before Income Tax but after National Insurance, depending on your scheme). Either way, you've technically earned that money first.

Salary sacrifice works differently. You agree with your employer to formally reduce your contractual salary, and in exchange, your employer pays the sacrificed amount directly into your pension as an employer contribution. On paper, you never earned that slice of salary at all — which is exactly why it's treated differently for tax purposes.

Because that money never counted as your income, you don't pay Income Tax on it. And — this is the part people miss — you don't pay employee National Insurance on it either.

## The actual savings, by tax band

Here's where it gets concrete. UK employee National Insurance (2026/27) is charged at 8% on earnings between £12,570 and £50,270, and 2% above that. Combine that with Income Tax at your marginal rate, and here's what salary sacrifice saves you on each pound diverted into your pension:

- **Basic-rate taxpayer:** 20% Income Tax + 8% NI = **28% saved**
- **Higher-rate taxpayer:** 40% Income Tax + 2% NI = **42% saved**

So if you're a higher-rate taxpayer sacrificing £5,000 of salary into your pension, you'd typically save around £2,100 in combined tax and NI — meaning the actual cost to your take-home pay is closer to £2,900, while the full £5,000 lands in your pension.

Your employer also saves National Insurance on the sacrificed amount (currently 15% employer NI above the relevant threshold). Some employers pass some or all of that saving back into your pension too — it's worth asking whether yours does, since it's not automatic.

## Where it gets unusually powerful: the £100k–£125,140 band

This is the detail that makes salary sacrifice worth understanding properly rather than skimming.

Once your income crosses £100,000, your tax-free Personal Allowance starts tapering away — you lose £1 of allowance for every £2 you earn above £100,000, until it's gone entirely at £125,140. Because you're simultaneously earning at 40% and losing tax-free allowance, the effective marginal tax rate across that £25,140 band works out to roughly 60%.

Salary sacrifice directly counters this. Because sacrificed salary reduces your taxable income, pulling your income back below £100,000 (or partway back) restores some or all of that lost Personal Allowance. In this band, each pound sacrificed can effectively be worth significantly more than the standard 42% higher-rate saving — some estimates put the effective relief in this range as high as 60%+ once the allowance restoration is factored in.

If your salary sits anywhere in that £100k–£125,140 range, this is genuinely one of the highest-value tax moves available to a UK employee, and it's worth running the actual numbers for your situation rather than assuming the standard 42% figure applies.

## The Child Benefit angle

If you claim Child Benefit and either you or your partner earns over £60,000, you'll be familiar with the High Income Child Benefit Charge (HICBC) — it claws back some or all of the benefit as your income rises through a £20,000 band, up to £80,000, where it's gone entirely.

Because salary sacrifice reduces your adjusted net income (the figure HICBC is based on), pulling your income down through sacrifice can reduce or eliminate the charge. Stack that on top of the 40% Income Tax and 2% NI you're already saving, and the effective value of sacrificing in this band can climb into the low-to-mid 50s percentage-wise for some households.

One practical note: even if HICBC makes it not worth *receiving* the Child Benefit payment, it's usually still worth staying registered for it — the claim protects National Insurance credits toward your State Pension for the claiming parent.

## What to check before you sign up

- **Your Annual Allowance.** Most people can contribute up to £60,000 a year across all pension contributions (yours plus your employer's, including whatever you sacrifice) before extra tax charges kick in. Unless you're a very high earner or already maxing contributions, this is unlikely to be a constraint — but it's worth a quick check if you're sacrificing a large chunk of a high salary.
- **Mortgage and benefit calculations.** Because salary sacrifice formally reduces your contractual salary, it can affect mortgage affordability assessments, statutory maternity/paternity pay, and other salary-linked calculations. Worth checking with your lender or HR if any of these apply to you before signing up for a large sacrifice.
- **The scheme rules change is coming.** From 6 April 2029, the government is introducing a cap on how much salary sacrifice can be exempt from National Insurance — the first £2,000 sacrificed per year will remain NI-free, but amounts sacrificed above that will start attracting NI again for both employee and employer. This doesn't affect the Income Tax saving, and it's a few years out, but if you're planning long-term around large sacrifice amounts, it's worth having on your radar.

## The short version

Salary sacrifice isn't a trick — it's simply routing money into your pension before it becomes "your income" in the eyes of HMRC, which means you avoid Income Tax and National Insurance on it rather than just Income Tax. For most people that's worth 28-42%. For people in the £100k-£125,140 taper band, or households affected by the Child Benefit charge, it can be worth meaningfully more. If your employer offers it and you haven't checked whether you're using it, that's a five-minute conversation with HR that's very likely worth having.
